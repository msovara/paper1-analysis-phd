## Abstract
African drought is a major climate hazard with severe societal and ecological impacts, yet its variability remains difficult to attribute and predict at regional scales. We investigate the influence of the Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) on African drought variability during 1980–2020 using standardized drought indices (SPI and SPEI at 3‑ and 6‑month accumulation) and a suite of diagnostics including event composites, ENSO-stratified composites, lag correlations, partial correlations, seasonal contrasts, and wavelet coherence. To connect statistical associations to plausible mechanisms, we analyze ERA5 atmospheric circulation composites during SON for canonical positive and negative IOD event years, evaluating mean sea-level pressure, 850‑hPa winds, and 500‑hPa geopotential height anomalies. Across Africa, drought responses to IOD phase are spatially heterogeneous and vary with drought metric and time scale, while ENSO state modulates both the magnitude and footprint of anomalies. Circulation composites reveal patterns consistent with dynamically driven changes in low-level moisture transport and mid‑tropospheric height anomalies during IOD events. Together, these results provide a physically coherent framework for incorporating IOD information into drought early warning and seasonal outlook guidance over Africa.

**Keywords:** Indian Ocean Dipole; drought; Africa; SPI; SPEI; ENSO; ERA5; teleconnections; composites

## 1. Introduction
Drought is among the most consequential climate hazards across Africa, affecting agriculture, food systems, water resources, health outcomes, and economic stability. The impacts of drought are often amplified by high climate sensitivity, limited adaptive capacity, and strong dependence on rain‑fed production. Improving drought attribution and predictability is therefore of high scientific and societal value. While local land–atmosphere feedbacks and regional circulation features shape drought development, large-scale ocean–atmosphere modes can impose predictable signals on rainfall and hydroclimate variability across wide regions.

The Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) is a key mode of coupled variability in the Indian Ocean, commonly quantified by the Dipole Mode Index (DMI), which measures the SST anomaly gradient between the western and eastern equatorial Indian Ocean (Saji et al., 1999; Webster et al., 1999). The IOD exhibits strong seasonal phase‑locking, typically peaking during boreal autumn (September–November; SON) (Saji et al., 1999; Cai et al., 2013). During positive IOD events, enhanced convection in the western Indian Ocean and suppressed convection in the eastern Indian Ocean reorganize tropical circulation, potentially altering moisture fluxes and rainfall across East Africa and beyond (Saji et al., 1999; Black et al., 2003). Conversely, negative IOD events reverse this pattern, with associated changes in convection, low-level winds, and pressure fields (Saji et al., 1999). However, the influence of IOD on African hydroclimate is not uniform; responses depend on region, season, and interactions with other modes such as ENSO (Black et al., 2003; Marchant et al., 2007).

ENSO and IOD can occur independently but often interact through shared Indo-Pacific circulation pathways (Webster et al., 1999; Cai et al., 2013). ENSO can influence the mean state and variability of the Indian Ocean and can amplify or suppress IOD-related convection anomalies (Cai et al., 2013). Consequently, statistical associations between DMI and drought indices may reflect direct IOD influence, ENSO co-variability, or state-dependent modulation. Furthermore, drought metrics differ: SPI reflects precipitation anomalies alone (McKee et al., 1993), while SPEI incorporates evaporative demand and can therefore express temperature-driven drying more strongly—an increasingly relevant distinction under warming (Vicente-Serrano et al., 2010; Dai, 2013).

This study combines multiple statistical perspectives and physically interpretable circulation diagnostics to evaluate IOD–drought relationships across Africa for 1980–2020. Specifically, we (i) quantify drought anomalies during IOD phases using SPI and SPEI at 3‑ and 6‑month time scales; (ii) assess ENSO modulation via stratified composites; (iii) examine timing and robustness using lag and partial correlations and wavelet coherence; and (iv) diagnose physically consistent circulation anomalies in ERA5 during canonical IOD events in SON using MSLP, 850‑hPa winds, and 500‑hPa geopotential height. The aim is to provide both statistical and mechanistic evidence supporting how IOD contributes to African drought variability and how that contribution is conditioned by ENSO and drought time scale.

## 2. Data and Methods
### 2.1 Study domain and analysis period
The analyses focus on Africa and the surrounding ocean regions that play an important role in supplying moisture to the continent. The study covers the period **1980–2020**, providing **492 monthly data points** for each climate index. To align with the time of year when the Indian Ocean Dipole is strongest, circulation patterns are examined primarily during the **September–October–November (SON) season**. The analysis spans the entire African continent (**35°S–37°N, 20°W–55°E**), and to better capture regional differences, the domain is subdivided into five regions: East Africa (10°S–20°N, 30°E–50°E), Southern Africa (35°S–10°S, 10°E–40°E), West Africa (5°S–20°N, 20°W–20°E), Central Africa (10°S–15°N, 5°E–30°E), and North Africa (15°N–37°N, 20°W–55°E).

### 2.2 Climate indices (IOD and ENSO)
IOD variability is represented by monthly DMI values (Saji et al., 1999). The Niño3.4 index represents ENSO variability. Months (or seasons) are classified into El Niño, La Niña, or Neutral conditions using a standard thresholding approach. **IOD events are identified when monthly DMI exceeds ±0.5°C, with positive IOD events defined as DMI > 0.5°C and negative IOD events as DMI < −0.5°C** (Saji et al., 1999). For ENSO classification, years are categorized based on SON-averaged Niño3.4 values: **El Niño when Niño3.4 ≥ +0.5°C, La Niña when Niño3.4 ≤ −0.5°C, and Neutral when −0.5°C < Niño3.4 < +0.5°C** (Trenberth, 1997). This seasonal averaging aligns with the IOD peak season and ensures consistency with the circulation composite analysis. Where relevant, analyses use these classifications to stratify IOD composites by ENSO state.

### 2.3 Drought indices (SPI and SPEI)
We use the Standardized Precipitation Index (SPI) and the Standardized Precipitation Evapotranspiration Index (SPEI) as standardised drought metrics. The SPI captures anomalies in rainfall alone (McKee et al., 1993), whereas the SPEI also accounts for atmospheric evaporative demand, making it more sensitive to temperature-driven drying (Vicente-Serrano et al., 2010). The SPI and SPEI indices are computed from precipitation and evapotranspiration anomalies at **3 and 6-month accumulation windows**. 

**SPI calculation**: For each grid point, precipitation is accumulated over the specified time window (3 or 6 months). The accumulated precipitation is then fitted to a **gamma distribution using the method of moments** (shape parameter α = μ²/σ², scale parameter β = σ²/μ, where μ is the mean and σ² is the variance). The cumulative probability from the fitted gamma distribution is transformed to a standard normal distribution to obtain SPI values (McKee et al., 1993). A minimum of 30 valid data points is required for reliable distribution fitting.

**SPEI calculation**: SPEI is computed from a climatic water balance (precipitation minus potential evapotranspiration, PET). PET is calculated using the **Thornthwaite method** (Thornthwaite, 1948) or similar temperature-based approach. The water balance (P − PET) is accumulated over the specified time window, and the accumulated values are fitted to a **log-logistic distribution** (Vicente-Serrano et al., 2010). The cumulative probability is then transformed to a standard normal distribution to obtain SPEI values. This approach allows SPEI to capture temperature-driven drying, as increased PET during warm periods can intensify drought even without precipitation deficits.

We analyse accumulation windows of **3 months (SPI3/SPEI3)** and **6 months (SPI6/SPEI6)**. These capture shorter-term moisture deficits and more persistent hydrological drought conditions, respectively. This distinction is increasingly important in a warming climate, where rising temperatures can intensify drought impacts even in the absence of large precipitation deficits (Dai, 2013).

### 2.4 Composite analysis
Composite drought anomalies are computed for positive and negative IOD phases by averaging drought index anomalies over the months associated with IOD events and comparing them to a climatological baseline. The climatology is calculated as the long-term mean (1980–2020) for the corresponding month or season, excluding the event years themselves to avoid circularity. For circulation composites, canonical SON IOD event years are used (Section 2.8). 

**Significance testing**: Statistical significance is assessed using a **two-tailed Student's t-test** at each grid point, comparing the composite mean (averaged across event years) to the climatological mean. The test statistic is calculated as *t* = (event_mean − climatology) / SE, where SE is the standard error of the mean (SE = event_std / √n_events, with ddof=1 for sample standard deviation). The degrees of freedom are *df* = n_events − 1. **Regions with p < 0.05 are considered statistically significant** and are indicated by stippling on composite maps. For circulation variables (e.g., MSLP), an additional practical significance threshold is applied (e.g., |anomaly| ≥ 0.5 hPa for MSLP) to filter out small but statistically significant anomalies that may not be meteorologically meaningful. No explicit multiple testing correction is applied, as the focus is on identifying coherent spatial patterns rather than individual grid-point significance.

### 2.5 ENSO-stratified composites
To evaluate state dependence, we compute composites conditional on ENSO state. A **3×3 framework** summarises drought anomalies for each combination of ENSO phase (El Niño, La Niña, Neutral) and IOD phase (positive, negative, and difference). This approach allows us to isolate the "pure" IOD signal under Neutral ENSO conditions and assess how ENSO modulates IOD–drought relationships. Where combinations have insufficient sample size (typically < 3 events), panels indicate low-data conditions rather than over-interpreting noisy composites. The stratification uses SON-averaged Niño3.4 values to classify each year's ENSO state, ensuring temporal alignment with the IOD peak season.

### 2.6 Lag and partial correlation analysis
Lag correlations are computed between DMI and drought indices across multiple lags from **−12 to +12 months** (25 total lags) to evaluate whether drought responses are concurrent, leading, or lagging relative to IOD variability. For each lag, Pearson correlation coefficients are calculated between the time-shifted DMI and drought index time series at each grid point or regional average. **Positive lags indicate DMI leading drought** (IOD forcing drought), while **negative lags indicate drought leading DMI** (interpreted cautiously as potential reverse causality or shared forcing). The analysis requires a minimum of 10 valid data pairs for each lag to ensure statistical reliability. The lag with the maximum absolute correlation is identified for each region/index combination to determine optimal lead times for prediction.

**Partial correlations** quantify the association between DMI and drought indices while controlling for ENSO (Niño3.4), providing a robustness check against ENSO confounding. The partial correlation *r*DMI,drought|ENSO is calculated as:

*r*DMI,drought|ENSO = (*r*DMI,drought − *r*DMI,ENSO × *r*drought,ENSO) / √[(1 − *r*²DMI,ENSO)(1 − *r*²drought,ENSO)]

where *r*DMI,drought, *r*DMI,ENSO, and *r*drought,ENSO are the pairwise Pearson correlations. This removes the linear influence of ENSO, allowing assessment of the independent IOD contribution to drought variability.

### 2.7 Wavelet coherence
Wavelet coherence is computed between DMI and drought indices to assess time–frequency dependence. This reveals whether coupling is persistent over the full record or concentrated in particular epochs and at particular periodicities (e.g., interannual 2–7‑year bands) (Torrence and Compo, 1998; Grinsted et al., 2004).

### 2.8 Atmospheric circulation composites (ERA5)
We use ERA5 to compute SON circulation anomalies during canonical IOD event years. Variables include:
- **MSLP (mean sea-level pressure)**: Surface pressure field indicating convergence/divergence patterns and pressure gradients
- **850‑hPa winds (u850, v850)**: Low-level horizontal wind components representing moisture transport pathways
- **500‑hPa geopotential height (z500, converted to meters)**: Mid-tropospheric height field indicating ridge/trough structures and vertical motion patterns

**Canonical IOD event years** used in the circulation composite workflow are selected based on SON-averaged DMI magnitude from HadISST data:
- **Positive IOD**: **1994, 1997, 2006, 2018, 2019** (n = 5 events)
- **Negative IOD**: **1980, 1981, 1992, 1996, 1998** (n = 5 events)

These years represent the strongest IOD events during the analysis period, ensuring robust composite signals. The selection prioritizes events with DMI magnitude > 0.5°C during SON, with the top 5 events chosen for each phase to balance signal strength and sample size.

**Composite methodology**: For each variable, SON-averaged values are extracted for each canonical event year. The climatology is calculated as the SON mean over all years in the period (1980–2020), **excluding the event years themselves** to prevent circularity. Anomalies are computed as the difference between the event-year composite mean and the climatology. The composite mean is the simple average across all events within each IOD phase (positive or negative). Statistical significance is assessed using the same t-test approach as for drought composites (Section 2.4), with p < 0.05 indicating significant anomalies.

**Visualization**: For interpretability, geopotential height anomalies and wind vectors are plotted separately, in addition to multi-variable circulation composite panels. This separation reduces visual clutter and allows clearer interpretation of each field. **Projection variants include Mercator** (suitable for equatorial and mid-latitude regions, extent: 180°W–180°E, 90°S–15°N) **and polar stereographic** (suitable for Southern Hemisphere focus, extent: 180°W–180°E, 90°S–0°N), with hemispheric extents to capture large-scale circulation patterns beyond the African continent.

ERA5 is the fifth-generation ECMWF atmospheric reanalysis, providing physically consistent fields at 0.25° × 0.25° horizontal resolution suitable for diagnosing circulation mechanisms (Hersbach et al., 2020).

## 3. Results
### 3.1 DMI dataset characterization and strong IOD events

Before examining drought teleconnections, we first characterize the DMI time series from three datasets (HadISST, ERSST, and ERA5) to assess inter-dataset agreement and identify canonical strong IOD events used in subsequent composite analyses.

**Monthly time series comparison (1980–2020)**: The full monthly DMI time series from all three datasets (HadISST, ERSST, and ERA5) reveal strong interannual variability with generally consistent timing of major events across the 492-month record, though with notable differences in magnitude (Figure 1a). All three series exhibit sharp peaks (positive DMI events) and deep troughs (negative DMI events) throughout the period, frequently crossing the ±0.5°C IOD threshold. HadISST and ERSST DMI values track very closely, showing similar magnitudes and timing for most events, with both series frequently exceeding the positive IOD threshold and occasionally dropping below the negative threshold. The ERA5 DMI series generally follows the same timing for major events as HadISST and ERSST but shows much larger amplitude variations, particularly for negative DMI events. ERA5's troughs are consistently much deeper, often falling below −1.5°C and sometimes below −2.0°C, while HadISST and ERSST rarely drop below −1.0°C. Prominent positive DMI events (exceeding +0.5°C) appear in all three series around 1982–1983, 1986–1987, 1994, 1997–1998 (a very strong event, with ERA5 exceeding 2°C), 2002–2003, 2007, 2011, 2015, and 2019 (another very strong event, with ERA5 exceeding 2°C and HadISST/ERSST reaching above 1.5°C). ERA5 shows numerous and very strong negative events, frequently dropping below −1.5°C (e.g., early 1980s, mid-1980s, early 1990s, late 1990s, mid-2000s, early 2010s, mid-2010s), while HadISST and ERSST show less frequent and less extreme negative excursions.

**SON-averaged time series and correlations**: When focusing on the SON season (when IOD typically peaks), the correlation patterns become clearer. HadISST and ERSST SON-averaged DMI values track closely throughout the period, with correlation coefficient *r* = 0.794 (*p* < 10⁻¹⁰⁸), indicating excellent agreement on both the timing and magnitude of IOD variability (Figure 1d). In contrast, correlations involving ERA5 are weaker but still highly significant: HadISST vs ERA5 (*r* = 0.572, *p* < 10⁻⁴⁴) and ERSST vs ERA5 (*r* = 0.580, *p* < 10⁻⁴⁵). The scatter plot relationships (Figure 1d) confirm these correlation patterns, with HadISST–ERSST showing tight clustering around the 1:1 line, while ERA5 comparisons show greater scatter and systematic biases.

**Distribution characteristics**: Box plot analysis reveals systematic differences in DMI distributions across datasets (Figure 1b). HadISST DMI has a median near zero (−0.05°C) with an interquartile range (IQR) from approximately −0.2°C to 0.05°C, and whiskers extending from −0.7°C to 0.7°C. ERSST DMI shows a similar distribution with median at 0.0°C, IQR from −0.1°C to 0.2°C, and whiskers from −0.5°C to 0.7°C. ERA5 DMI exhibits a notably different distribution with a more negative median (−0.4°C), wider IQR (−0.8°C to −0.05°C), and much broader whiskers extending from approximately −2.2°C to 1.2°C. These differences reflect the reanalysis nature of ERA5, which may capture more extreme variability or incorporate different observational constraints compared to the observational SST products. Monthly box plots for HadISST (Figure 1c) show that DMI variability is relatively uniform across months, with medians near zero and similar IQRs, though some months (e.g., September–November) show slightly wider distributions consistent with IOD peak seasonality.

**Strong IOD events**: The top 5 positive IOD events identified from SON-averaged HadISST DMI are 1994, 1997, 2006, 2018, and 2019 (Figure 2a). The top 5 negative events are 1980, 1981, 1992, 1996, and 1998 (Figure 2b). These canonical events are used for circulation composite analysis (Section 3.8). Comparison across datasets reveals important inter-dataset differences in event magnitude. For positive events, ERA5 consistently shows higher DMI values than HadISST and ERSST, particularly in 1994 (ERA5 ~1.2°C vs HadISST ~0.5°C) and 1997 (ERA5 ~1.7°C vs HadISST ~1.0°C). ERSST often shows the weakest positive signals, with some events (1994, 2006, 2018) falling below the +0.5°C threshold or even negative. For negative events, ERA5 again shows stronger (more negative) values, particularly in 1996 (ERA5 ~−1.2°C vs HadISST ~−0.7°C) and 1992 (ERA5 ~−0.8°C vs HadISST ~−0.6°C). ERSST shows the weakest negative signals, with some events (1980, 1981, 1992) barely exceeding the −0.5°C threshold.

**Inter-dataset scatter relationships**: Scatter plots comparing strong events across datasets (Figure 2c–d) reveal systematic biases. HadISST vs ERSST comparisons show that for positive events, ERSST values are generally lower than HadISST (points below the 1:1 line), while for negative events, ERSST values are less negative than HadISST (points above the 1:1 line). In contrast, HadISST vs ERA5 comparisons show the opposite pattern: ERA5 values are generally higher than HadISST for positive events (points above the 1:1 line) and more negative for negative events (points below the 1:1 line). These biases are consistent with ERA5's tendency to amplify IOD extremes, which may reflect the reanalysis's incorporation of atmospheric observations that respond to SST gradients, or differences in how SST anomalies are represented in reanalysis versus observational products.

**Implications for analysis**: The inter-dataset differences highlight the importance of dataset choice in IOD studies. HadISST serves as the reference dataset for event identification in this study, as it is widely used in IOD research and shows good agreement with ERSST. ERA5's stronger amplitude variations may provide enhanced signal-to-noise for circulation composites, but may also introduce artifacts if the reanalysis over-amplifies extremes. The consistent timing of events across all three datasets provides confidence that the identified canonical years represent genuine IOD variability rather than dataset-specific artifacts.

### 3.2 Drought composites by IOD phase

Composite drought maps reveal spatially heterogeneous responses across Africa that vary systematically by index type (SPI vs SPEI) and accumulation window (3 vs 6 months). The following subsections provide detailed annotations for each drought index, highlighting both commonalities and distinctions in how IOD phases manifest across different drought metrics.

#### 3.2.1 SPI3 (3-month Standardized Precipitation Index)

SPI3 composites capture short-term precipitation anomalies, reflecting moisture deficits that develop over a 3-month accumulation period. Each composite figure (Figure 3a) contains four panels: (1) positive IOD composite showing average SPI3 anomalies during positive IOD events, (2) negative IOD composite showing average SPI3 anomalies during negative IOD events, (3) difference map (positive minus negative IOD) highlighting regions where IOD phase has the strongest differential impact, and (4) regional averages bar chart comparing the five African regions.

**Positive IOD composite:** During positive IOD events, SPI3 shows spatially heterogeneous patterns with generally wetter-than-normal conditions across most of Africa, except Southern Africa which experiences drier conditions. The spatial pattern reveals enhanced precipitation in East Africa, West Africa, Central Africa, and particularly North Africa, with statistically significant anomalies (p < 0.05, indicated by stippling) covering approximately **16.3%** of grid points. Regional averages from the bar chart indicate: **East Africa +0.574**, **Southern Africa −0.182**, **West Africa +0.487**, **Central Africa +0.278**, and **North Africa +0.869**. The strongest wet signals (positive SPI3 values > +0.5) appear in North Africa, East Africa, and West Africa, indicating enhanced precipitation during positive IOD events. In contrast, Southern Africa shows moderate drought conditions (SPI3 = −0.182), suggesting reduced precipitation in this region during positive IOD phases.

**Negative IOD composite:** Negative IOD events are associated with generally weaker and more spatially variable patterns relative to positive IOD conditions. Statistically significant anomalies cover approximately **12.6%** of grid points, indicating a somewhat weaker signal than positive IOD events. Regional averages indicate: **East Africa +0.163**, **Southern Africa +0.109**, **West Africa +0.191**, **Central Africa +0.474**, and **North Africa −0.152**. Notably, Central Africa shows the strongest positive anomaly (+0.474) during negative IOD events, while North Africa experiences slight drought conditions (SPI3 = −0.152). The difference map (positive minus negative) reveals that North Africa shows the largest differential response (+1.021), indicating strong asymmetry between positive and negative IOD effects, with positive IOD events bringing substantially more precipitation to this region. East Africa also shows a notable difference (+0.411), while Central Africa shows an opposite pattern, with negative IOD events associated with wetter conditions than positive IOD events.

**Key features:**
- Captures short-term (3-month) precipitation deficits
- Most sensitive to seasonal rainfall variations
- Strongest signals in regions with direct Indian Ocean moisture influence
- Anomalies can develop and decay relatively quickly

#### 3.2.2 SPI6 (6-month Standardized Precipitation Index)

SPI6 composites integrate precipitation anomalies over a 6-month accumulation period, providing a measure of more persistent hydrological drought conditions. Compared to SPI3, SPI6 anomalies typically show similar spatial patterns but with slightly enhanced magnitudes in most regions, reflecting the cumulative nature of the 6-month accumulation window. The longer accumulation window means SPI6 reflects cumulative moisture deficits that may persist beyond the immediate IOD forcing period, incorporating lagged responses and soil moisture memory effects. During positive IOD events, SPI6 shows regional averages of: **East Africa +0.668**, **Southern Africa −0.135**, **West Africa +0.374**, **Central Africa +0.249**, and **North Africa +0.878**. Statistically significant anomalies cover approximately **16.1%** of grid points. The 6-month integration means these anomalies may extend beyond the SON season, capturing both direct IOD forcing and subsequent persistence. Notably, East Africa shows a stronger positive signal in SPI6 (+0.668) compared to SPI3 (+0.574), suggesting that positive IOD impacts accumulate over the longer time window. North Africa maintains the strongest positive anomaly (+0.878), similar to SPI3, indicating consistent enhancement of precipitation during positive IOD events. Negative IOD events are associated with regional averages of: **East Africa +0.298**, **Southern Africa −0.165**, **West Africa +0.329**, **Central Africa +0.609**, and **North Africa −0.070**. Statistically significant anomalies cover approximately **11.2%** of grid points. Central Africa shows the strongest positive anomaly (+0.609) during negative IOD events, even stronger than in SPI3, suggesting that negative IOD impacts on Central Africa precipitation accumulate over the 6-month window. The persistence of SPI6 anomalies relative to SPI3 suggests that some IOD-related drought impacts accumulate over time, with the longer window capturing both direct forcing and subsequent persistence mechanisms.

**Key features:**
- Captures medium-term (6-month) precipitation deficits
- Reflects cumulative and persistent hydrological drought
- Incorporates lagged responses and soil moisture memory
- Anomalies may persist beyond immediate IOD forcing period

#### 3.2.3 SPEI3 (3-month Standardized Precipitation Evapotranspiration Index)

SPEI3 composites incorporate both precipitation and evaporative demand, making this index sensitive to temperature-driven drying in addition to precipitation deficits. During positive IOD events, SPEI3 shows regional averages of: **East Africa +0.391**, **Southern Africa −0.293**, **West Africa +0.435**, **Central Africa +0.259**, and **North Africa +0.648**. Statistically significant anomalies cover approximately **6.2%** of grid points, notably lower than SPI3 (16.3%), suggesting that evaporative demand effects may reduce the spatial coherence of the signal. Notable differences from SPI3 appear in several regions: East Africa shows weaker positive anomalies in SPEI3 (+0.391) compared to SPI3 (+0.574), suggesting that increased evaporative demand partially offsets precipitation gains. Southern Africa shows stronger drought conditions in SPEI3 (−0.293) compared to SPI3 (−0.182), indicating that evaporative demand amplifies the precipitation deficit in this region. West Africa shows similar values between SPEI3 (+0.435) and SPI3 (+0.487), suggesting that precipitation effects dominate. North Africa shows weaker positive anomalies in SPEI3 (+0.648) compared to SPI3 (+0.869), indicating that evaporative demand reduces the net water balance benefit despite enhanced precipitation. In semi-arid and arid zones, SPEI3 anomalies are often weaker than SPI3, reflecting the additional contribution of atmospheric evaporative demand that partially offsets precipitation gains. The 3-month accumulation window means SPEI3 captures short-term water balance anomalies, where increased temperature and evaporative demand during IOD events can intensify drought even in the absence of large precipitation deficits. During negative IOD events, SPEI3 shows regional averages of: **East Africa +0.320**, **Southern Africa +0.234**, **West Africa +0.278**, **Central Africa +0.546**, and **North Africa +0.091**. Statistically significant anomalies cover approximately **13.0%** of grid points, higher than for positive IOD events. Patterns differ from SPI3 in several regions: Southern Africa shows positive anomalies in SPEI3 (+0.234) compared to slightly positive in SPI3 (+0.109), suggesting that reduced evaporative demand during negative IOD events enhances water availability. Central Africa shows similar strong positive anomalies in both indices, indicating that precipitation effects dominate. North Africa shows positive anomalies in SPEI3 (+0.091) compared to negative in SPI3 (−0.152), suggesting that reduced evaporative demand during negative IOD events improves water balance despite reduced precipitation.

**Key features:**
- Captures short-term (3-month) water balance (precipitation minus PET)
- Sensitive to both precipitation deficits and temperature-driven evaporative demand
- Shows enhanced drying in semi-arid/arid regions relative to SPI3
- Reflects combined effects of rainfall anomalies and atmospheric moisture demand

#### 3.2.4 SPEI6 (6-month Standardized Precipitation Evapotranspiration Index)

SPEI6 composites integrate water balance anomalies over a 6-month period, providing the most comprehensive measure of persistent drought conditions that account for both precipitation and evaporative demand. Compared to SPEI3, SPEI6 shows generally stronger positive anomalies in most regions, reflecting the cumulative nature of the 6-month accumulation window. The 6-month accumulation window means SPEI6 captures cumulative water balance deficits that incorporate both precipitation anomalies and sustained periods of elevated evaporative demand. During positive IOD events, SPEI6 shows regional averages of: **East Africa +0.524**, **Southern Africa −0.247**, **West Africa +0.354**, **Central Africa +0.234**, and **North Africa +0.579**. Statistically significant anomalies cover approximately **8.4%** of grid points. The anomalies reflect the combined effects of precipitation and evaporative demand over extended periods. Compared to SPI6, SPEI6 shows weaker positive anomalies in all regions except Southern Africa (where both show negative values), indicating that cumulative evaporative demand partially offsets precipitation gains over the 6-month window. East Africa shows stronger positive anomalies in SPEI6 (+0.524) compared to SPEI3 (+0.391), suggesting that positive IOD impacts accumulate over time. Southern Africa shows persistent drought conditions (−0.247), similar to SPEI3, indicating sustained water deficits. Negative IOD events are associated with regional averages of: **East Africa +0.423**, **Southern Africa −0.020**, **West Africa +0.456**, **Central Africa +0.696**, and **North Africa +0.190**. Statistically significant anomalies cover approximately **13.0%** of grid points. Central Africa shows the strongest positive anomaly (+0.696), even stronger than in SPEI3, suggesting that negative IOD impacts on Central Africa water balance accumulate substantially over the 6-month window. Southern Africa shows near-neutral conditions (−0.020) in SPEI6 compared to positive in SPEI3 (+0.234), suggesting that cumulative effects differ from short-term responses. Differences between SPEI6 and SPI6 are most pronounced in regions where temperature anomalies and evaporative demand contribute substantially to drought development. #### 3.3.5 Synthesis and cross-index comparisons

**ENSO modulation patterns:** Across all indices, several consistent patterns emerge regarding ENSO modulation of IOD–drought relationships:

1. **Synergistic effects**: When IOD and ENSO phases align (e.g., positive IOD + El Niño, negative IOD + La Niña), their combined influence amplifies drought anomalies, resulting in stronger signals than IOD alone. During El Niño, positive IOD events (n=4: 1994, 1997, 2006, 2018) show enhanced wet conditions in North Africa (SPI3: +0.924, SPI6: +0.835) and East Africa (SPI3: +0.408, SPI6: +0.496), with statistically significant regions covering 12.2% of grid points for SPI indices. During La Niña, the single negative IOD event (n=1: 1998) shows enhanced wet conditions in West Africa (SPI3: +0.768) and Central Africa (SPI3: +0.792), though the single-event limitation precludes statistical significance. This reflects shared Indo-Pacific circulation pathways where both modes reinforce convection anomalies.

2. **Phase separation**: The analysis reveals a strong phase separation between IOD and ENSO: positive IOD events occur primarily during El Niño or Neutral conditions, while negative IOD events occur primarily during La Niña or Neutral conditions. This separation means that competing phase combinations (positive IOD + La Niña, negative IOD + El Niño) did not occur during 1980–2020, limiting our ability to assess cancellation effects directly. The observed patterns suggest that IOD and ENSO tend to co-occur in synergistic rather than competing configurations.

3. **Neutral ENSO baseline**: Under Neutral ENSO conditions, the "pure" IOD signal is most clearly visible, providing a baseline for assessing ENSO's modifying influence. For negative IOD under Neutral conditions (n=4: 1980, 1981, 1992, 1996), composites show statistically significant patterns (11.2–15.1% of grid points depending on index), with enhanced precipitation in Central Africa (SPI3: +0.315, SPI6: +0.531) and East Africa (SPI3: +0.119, SPI6: +0.241). For positive IOD under Neutral conditions (n=1: 2019), only one event occurred, limiting statistical robustness, but the pattern shows enhanced precipitation in East Africa (SPI3: +0.973) and drought in Southern Africa (SPI3: −1.087), aligning with the overall positive IOD composite from Section 3.2.

**Index-specific ENSO modulation:**
- **SPI indices**: ENSO modulation primarily affects precipitation anomalies, with El Niño and La Niña modifying IOD-related rainfall patterns through changes in Indo-Pacific convection and the Walker circulation.
- **SPEI indices**: ENSO modulation affects both precipitation and evaporative demand. El Niño conditions, often associated with elevated temperatures, may amplify drought conditions in SPEI relative to SPI, while La Niña conditions, often associated with cooler temperatures, may enhance water balance benefits.

**Accumulation window effects:** Comparing 3-month and 6-month indices under the same ENSO×IOD combination reveals how accumulation windows affect ENSO modulation. In general, 6-month indices show similar or stronger signals than 3-month indices, as cumulative effects accumulate over the longer time window. For example, during El Niño, SPI6 shows stronger positive anomalies than SPI3 in East Africa (+0.496 vs +0.408), while during Neutral conditions, negative IOD shows stronger positive anomalies in SPI6 than SPI3 in Central Africa (+0.531 vs +0.315) and East Africa (+0.241 vs +0.119). However, the pattern of ENSO modulation (synergistic effects, phase separation) is generally consistent across accumulation windows, with both 3-month and 6-month indices showing the same phase separation patterns.

**Regional heterogeneity:** ENSO modulation of IOD–drought relationships exhibits spatial heterogeneity across Africa. Regions directly influenced by Indian Ocean moisture transport (e.g., East Africa) show consistent positive anomalies during positive IOD events under both El Niño (+0.408 in SPI3) and Neutral (+0.973 in SPI3) conditions, though the Neutral result is based on a single event. North Africa shows the strongest positive anomalies during positive IOD + El Niño (+0.924 in SPI3, +0.835 in SPI6), suggesting strong synergistic effects in this region. Southern Africa shows contrasting patterns: during positive IOD + El Niño, conditions are near-neutral (+0.054 in SPI3), while during positive IOD + Neutral, strong drought occurs (−1.087 in SPI3), though the Neutral result is based on a single event. Central Africa shows enhanced wet conditions during negative IOD events under both La Niña (+0.792 in SPI3) and Neutral (+0.315 in SPI3) conditions, with stronger signals in the 6-month accumulation window (+0.531 in SPI6 under Neutral). This heterogeneity reflects differences in moisture source regions, regional circulation regimes, and the pathways by which ENSO×IOD interactions project onto African hydroclimate.

**Sample size limitations:** The ENSO-stratified analysis reveals important limitations related to sample sizes. Some ENSO×IOD combinations have very few events: positive IOD + Neutral (n=1: 2019), negative IOD + La Niña (n=1: 1998), and no events for competing combinations (positive IOD + La Niña, negative IOD + El Niño). These small sample sizes limit statistical robustness and require cautious interpretation. The single-event composites (positive IOD + Neutral, negative IOD + La Niña) show patterns that align with overall IOD composites but should not be over-interpreted. The phase separation between IOD and ENSO (positive IOD with El Niño/Neutral, negative IOD with La Niña/Neutral) means that competing phase combinations are rare or absent, which is itself an important finding about IOD–ENSO co-occurrence patterns.

**Implications for forecasting:** The ENSO-stratified results have important implications for operational drought forecasting. When both IOD and ENSO states are known (e.g., from seasonal climate models), forecasters can use the appropriate ENSO×IOD combination to provide more accurate drought outlooks. The results indicate that IOD impacts cannot be interpreted independently of ENSO state, and forecast systems should consider both modes jointly rather than treating DMI as an isolated predictor. However, the phase separation observed (positive IOD with El Niño, negative IOD with La Niña) means that forecasters should be aware that certain phase combinations are more likely than others, which can inform probabilistic drought outlooks.

### 3.4 Timing of associations (lag correlations)

Lag correlation analysis reveals distinct temporal relationships between IOD and African drought that vary systematically by region, index type, and accumulation window. The analysis examines correlations across lags from **−12 to +12 months** (25 total lags), where positive lags indicate DMI leading drought (IOD forcing drought) and negative lags indicate drought leading DMI (interpreted cautiously as potential reverse causality or shared forcing). The results provide insights into optimal lead times for prediction and the mechanisms underlying IOD–drought teleconnections.

#### 3.4.1 Regional patterns in lag correlations

**East Africa:** East Africa shows the strongest concurrent correlations (zero lag) across all indices, indicating that IOD and drought are most tightly coupled at the same time. For **SPI3**, the maximum correlation is **0.169 at lag ±1 months** (p < 0.001), with the zero-lag correlation of **0.167** (p < 0.001). The correlation pattern is symmetric around zero lag, with significant correlations (p < 0.05) at 11 out of 25 lags, including lags at ±3 months (r = 0.129, p = 0.004). For **SPI6**, the pattern is similar but stronger, with maximum correlation of **0.226 at lag ±1 months** (p < 0.001) and zero-lag correlation of **0.208** (p < 0.001). The 6-month accumulation window shows enhanced correlations compared to SPI3, reflecting cumulative effects. For **SPEI3**, the maximum correlation occurs at **zero lag (r = 0.161, p < 0.001)**, with significant correlations at 23 out of 25 lags, indicating persistent coupling across multiple time scales. For **SPEI6**, the pattern is similar, with maximum correlation at **zero lag (r = 0.176, p < 0.001)** and significant correlations at 21 out of 25 lags. The strong concurrent correlations in East Africa are consistent with direct Indian Ocean moisture transport during SON, when IOD peaks and East African short rains occur.

**Southern Africa:** Southern Africa shows a distinctly different pattern, with peak correlations occurring at **±9 months** rather than zero lag, suggesting either delayed responses or shared forcing mechanisms. For **SPI3**, the maximum correlation is **0.209 at lag ±9 months** (p < 0.001), while the zero-lag correlation is near-zero (**−0.007, p = 0.885**), indicating no concurrent relationship. Significant correlations occur at 14 out of 25 lags, with notable correlations at ±6 months (r = 0.094, p = 0.039). For **SPI6**, the pattern is similar but stronger, with maximum correlation of **0.227 at lag ±9 months** (p < 0.001) and zero-lag correlation of **−0.035** (p = 0.442). Significant correlations occur at 16 out of 25 lags, including strong correlations at ±6 months (r = 0.167, p < 0.001). For **SPEI3**, the maximum correlation is **0.241 at lag ±9 months** (p < 0.001), with zero-lag correlation of **−0.046** (p = 0.306). Significant correlations occur at 16 out of 25 lags, with strong correlations at ±6 months (r = 0.164, p < 0.001). For **SPEI6**, the maximum correlation is **0.256 at lag ±8 months** (p < 0.001), with zero-lag correlation of **−0.052** (p = 0.246). Significant correlations occur at 18 out of 25 lags, with very strong correlations at ±6 months (r = 0.245, p < 0.001). The ±9-month lag pattern in Southern Africa suggests that IOD-related drought impacts may be delayed by approximately one seasonal cycle, or that both IOD and drought respond to shared forcing with a 9-month phase difference. The stronger correlations in SPEI indices compared to SPI indices suggest that evaporative demand effects may contribute to the delayed response.

**West Africa:** West Africa shows negative correlations with peak values at **±3 to ±4 months**, indicating an inverse relationship between IOD and drought. For **SPI3**, the maximum (absolute) correlation is **−0.156 at lag ±4 months** (p < 0.001), with zero-lag correlation of **−0.029** (p = 0.525). Significant correlations occur at 10 out of 25 lags, with notable correlations at ±3 months (r = −0.149, p = 0.001) and ±6 months (r = −0.106, p = 0.019). For **SPI6**, the pattern is stronger, with maximum correlation of **−0.186 at lag ±4 months** (p < 0.001) and zero-lag correlation of **−0.086** (p = 0.057). Significant correlations occur at 14 out of 25 lags, with strong correlations at ±3 months (r = −0.179, p < 0.001) and ±6 months (r = −0.142, p = 0.002). For **SPEI3**, the maximum correlation is **−0.089 at lag ±3 months** (p = 0.050), with zero-lag correlation of **−0.017** (p = 0.700). Significant correlations occur at only 2 out of 25 lags, indicating weaker coupling in water balance terms. For **SPEI6**, the maximum correlation is **−0.110 at lag ±3 months** (p = 0.015), with zero-lag correlation of **−0.077** (p = 0.088). Significant correlations occur at 4 out of 25 lags. The negative correlations in West Africa suggest that positive IOD events are associated with reduced drought (wetter conditions), while negative IOD events are associated with increased drought. The ±3–4 month lag suggests that the relationship may be mediated through delayed circulation responses or seasonal transitions.

**Central Africa:** Central Africa shows the weakest correlations overall, with no statistically significant correlations for most indices. For **SPI3**, the maximum correlation is **0.054 at lag ±12 months** (p = 0.234), with zero-lag correlation of **0.036** (p = 0.428). No significant correlations occur at any lag. For **SPI6**, the maximum correlation is **0.095 at lag ±12 months** (p = 0.038), with zero-lag correlation of **0.021** (p = 0.645). Significant correlations occur at only 2 out of 25 lags. For **SPEI3**, the maximum correlation is **0.053 at zero lag** (p = 0.237), with no significant correlations. For **SPEI6**, the maximum correlation is **0.071 at lag ±12 months** (p = 0.121), with zero-lag correlation of **0.017** (p = 0.702). No significant correlations occur. The weak correlations in Central Africa suggest that IOD has minimal direct influence on drought in this region, or that the relationship is masked by other factors such as local land–atmosphere feedbacks or competing teleconnections.

**North Africa:** North Africa shows moderate correlations that vary by index type. For **SPI3**, the maximum correlation is **0.053 at lag ±7 months** (p = 0.247), with zero-lag correlation of **0.051** (p = 0.260). No significant correlations occur. For **SPI6**, the maximum correlation is **0.049 at lag ±10 months** (p = 0.287), with zero-lag correlation of **0.032** (p = 0.476). No significant correlations occur. For **SPEI3**, the maximum correlation is **0.125 at zero lag** (p = 0.006), with significant correlations at 17 out of 25 lags, including lags at ±3 months (r = 0.090, p = 0.046) and ±6 months (r = 0.114, p = 0.012). For **SPEI6**, the maximum correlation is **0.125 at lag ±2 months** (p = 0.006), with zero-lag correlation of **0.125** (p = 0.006). Significant correlations occur at 19 out of 25 lags, including lags at ±3 months (r = 0.111, p = 0.014) and ±6 months (r = 0.115, p = 0.011). The stronger correlations in SPEI indices compared to SPI indices in North Africa suggest that evaporative demand effects contribute substantially to IOD–drought relationships in this region, with concurrent coupling (zero lag) being most important.

#### 3.4.2 Index-specific patterns

**SPI3 vs SPI6:** Comparing 3-month and 6-month precipitation indices reveals that SPI6 generally shows stronger correlations than SPI3, reflecting cumulative effects over the longer accumulation window. In East Africa, SPI6 shows stronger zero-lag correlations (0.208 vs 0.167) and maximum correlations (0.226 vs 0.169). In Southern Africa, SPI6 shows stronger maximum correlations (0.227 vs 0.209) and more significant lags (16 vs 14). In West Africa, SPI6 shows stronger negative correlations (−0.186 vs −0.156) and more significant lags (14 vs 10). The 6-month accumulation window captures cumulative precipitation effects that may persist beyond the immediate IOD forcing period, resulting in enhanced correlations.

**SPEI3 vs SPEI6:** Similar to SPI, SPEI6 generally shows stronger or similar correlations compared to SPEI3, with the 6-month accumulation capturing cumulative water balance effects. In East Africa, SPEI6 shows stronger zero-lag correlations (0.176 vs 0.161) and more significant lags (21 vs 23, though both are high). In Southern Africa, SPEI6 shows stronger maximum correlations (0.256 vs 0.241) and more significant lags (18 vs 16). In West Africa, SPEI6 shows stronger negative correlations (−0.110 vs −0.089) and more significant lags (4 vs 2). The 6-month accumulation means that both precipitation and evaporative demand effects accumulate over time, enhancing the IOD–drought coupling.

**SPI vs SPEI:** Comparing precipitation-only (SPI) and water balance (SPEI) indices reveals important differences in lag correlation patterns. In East Africa, SPI indices show peak correlations at ±1 month, while SPEI indices show peak correlations at zero lag, suggesting that evaporative demand effects enhance concurrent coupling. In Southern Africa, both SPI and SPEI show similar ±9-month lag patterns, but SPEI shows stronger correlations (0.241–0.256 vs 0.209–0.227), suggesting that evaporative demand contributes to the delayed response. In West Africa, both SPI and SPEI show negative correlations, but SPI shows stronger negative correlations than SPEI, suggesting that precipitation effects dominate the inverse relationship. In North Africa, SPEI shows much stronger correlations than SPI (0.125 vs 0.051–0.053), with significant correlations in SPEI but not in SPI, indicating that evaporative demand is a key mechanism linking IOD to drought in this region.

#### 3.4.3 Interpretation of lag patterns

**Concurrent coupling (zero lag):** Regions showing peak correlations at zero lag (East Africa for SPEI indices, North Africa for SPEI indices) are consistent with direct IOD forcing during SON, when IOD peaks and regional rainfall occurs. The strong concurrent correlations suggest that IOD-related circulation anomalies directly influence moisture transport and precipitation during the same season.

**Positive lags (IOD leading drought):** Positive lags indicate that IOD variability precedes drought responses, consistent with causal forcing. However, in this analysis, the maximum correlations often occur at symmetric positive and negative lags (e.g., ±1 month, ±3 months, ±9 months), making it difficult to distinguish between IOD leading drought and shared forcing mechanisms. The symmetric patterns suggest that the relationships may reflect shared seasonal cycles or common forcing rather than unidirectional causality.

**Negative lags (drought leading IOD):** Negative lags indicate that drought variability precedes IOD, which could reflect reverse causality, shared forcing, or chance alignment. The symmetric patterns observed (e.g., ±9 months in Southern Africa) suggest that negative lags should be interpreted cautiously, as they may reflect shared forcing mechanisms rather than drought causing IOD variability.

**Optimal lead times for prediction:** Based on maximum absolute correlations, optimal lead times vary by region and index. East Africa shows optimal lead times of **0 to ±1 months**, suggesting that IOD can provide very short-term (concurrent to 1-month lead) drought prediction. Southern Africa shows optimal lead times of **±8 to ±9 months**, suggesting potential for seasonal-scale prediction, though the symmetric pattern requires cautious interpretation. West Africa shows optimal lead times of **±3 to ±4 months**, suggesting sub-seasonal prediction potential. Central Africa shows weak correlations overall, limiting prediction potential. North Africa shows optimal lead times of **0 to ±2 months** for SPEI indices, suggesting short-term prediction potential.

### 3.5 Robustness controlling for ENSO (partial correlations)

Partial correlation analysis quantifies the independent contribution of IOD to African drought variability by controlling for ENSO (Niño3.4). This diagnostic removes the linear influence of ENSO, allowing assessment of whether IOD–drought relationships reflect direct IOD forcing or ENSO co-variability. The analysis compares raw correlations (DMI–drought) with partial correlations (DMI–drought | ENSO) to determine the degree of IOD independence from ENSO.

#### 3.5.1 Regional patterns in partial correlations

**East Africa:** East Africa shows high IOD independence from ENSO, with partial correlations retaining **80–88%** of the raw correlation strength across all indices. For **SPI3**, the raw correlation is **r = 0.167 (p < 0.001)**, while the partial correlation is **r = 0.134 (p = 0.003)**, indicating that **80.0%** of the IOD–drought relationship is independent of ENSO. The correlation reduction is only **0.033 (20.0% reduction)**, suggesting minimal ENSO confounding. For **SPI6**, the raw correlation is **r = 0.208 (p < 0.001)**, while the partial correlation is **r = 0.183 (p < 0.001)**, with **88.0%** independence. For **SPEI3**, the raw correlation is **r = 0.161 (p < 0.001)**, while the partial correlation is **r = 0.135 (p = 0.003)**, with **83.9%** independence. For **SPEI6**, the raw correlation is **r = 0.176 (p < 0.001)**, while the partial correlation is **r = 0.152 (p < 0.001)**, with **86.7%** independence. The high independence in East Africa supports a direct IOD–drought teleconnection, consistent with the region's proximity to the Indian Ocean and direct moisture transport pathways. The DMI–Niño3.4 correlation is **r = 0.163**, while drought–Niño3.4 correlations range from **r = 0.169 to 0.239**, indicating moderate ENSO influence on both IOD and drought, but the partial correlation analysis shows that IOD's influence on drought is largely independent.

**Southern Africa:** Southern Africa shows low to moderate IOD independence from ENSO, with partial correlations showing substantial reductions relative to raw correlations. For **SPI3**, the raw correlation is **r = −0.007 (p = 0.885)**, while the partial correlation is **r = 0.020 (p = 0.655)**. The near-zero raw correlation makes interpretation difficult, but the sign change suggests ENSO confounding. For **SPI6**, the raw correlation is **r = −0.035 (p = 0.442)**, while the partial correlation is **r = −0.002 (p = 0.973)**, with only **4.5%** of the raw correlation remaining. This indicates that **95.5%** of the apparent IOD–drought relationship is explained by ENSO co-variability, suggesting strong ENSO dependence. For **SPEI3**, the raw correlation is **r = −0.046 (p = 0.306)**, while the partial correlation is **r = −0.016 (p = 0.727)**, with **34.2%** independence. For **SPEI6**, the raw correlation is **r = −0.052 (p = 0.246)**, while the partial correlation is **r = −0.022 (p = 0.624)**, with **42.3%** independence. The drought–Niño3.4 correlations are negative (**r = −0.161 to −0.203**), indicating that ENSO and drought are inversely related in Southern Africa, which contributes to the ENSO confounding. The low independence suggests that IOD–drought relationships in Southern Africa are strongly mediated by ENSO, consistent with the region's indirect teleconnection pathways.

**West Africa:** West Africa shows moderate to high IOD independence from ENSO, with partial correlations retaining **58–81%** of the raw correlation strength. For **SPI3**, the raw correlation is **r = −0.029 (p = 0.525)**, while the partial correlation is **r = −0.021 (p = 0.649)**, with **71.7%** independence. For **SPI6**, the raw correlation is **r = −0.086 (p = 0.057)**, while the partial correlation is **r = −0.069 (p = 0.127)**, with **80.4%** independence. For **SPEI3**, the raw correlation is **r = −0.017 (p = 0.700)**, while the partial correlation is **r = −0.010 (p = 0.823)**, with **58.0%** independence. For **SPEI6**, the raw correlation is **r = −0.077 (p = 0.088)**, while the partial correlation is **r = −0.062 (p = 0.168)**, with **81.1%** independence. The moderate to high independence suggests that IOD has a partially independent influence on West African drought, though the weak raw correlations limit the practical significance of this finding. The drought–Niño3.4 correlations are weak negative (**r = −0.046 to −0.112**), indicating minimal direct ENSO influence on West African drought.

**Central Africa:** Central Africa shows high IOD independence from ENSO, with partial correlations often showing similar or slightly stronger values than raw correlations. For **SPI3**, the raw correlation is **r = 0.036 (p = 0.428)**, while the partial correlation is **r = 0.037 (p = 0.413)**, with **103.4%** independence (partial correlation is slightly stronger). For **SPI6**, the raw correlation is **r = 0.021 (p = 0.645)**, while the partial correlation is **r = 0.030 (p = 0.500)**, with **146.2%** independence. For **SPEI3**, the raw correlation is **r = 0.053 (p = 0.237)**, while the partial correlation is **r = 0.053 (p = 0.244)**, with **98.7%** independence. For **SPEI6**, the raw correlation is **r = 0.017 (p = 0.702)**, while the partial correlation is **r = 0.024 (p = 0.598)**, with **138.0%** independence. The high independence (often >100%) suggests that ENSO may actually suppress the IOD–drought relationship in Central Africa, with the partial correlation revealing a stronger independent IOD signal. The drought–Niño3.4 correlations are very weak (**r = −0.056 to 0.009**), indicating minimal direct ENSO influence on Central African drought, which explains the high IOD independence.

**North Africa:** North Africa shows low to moderate IOD independence from ENSO, with partial correlations showing substantial reductions relative to raw correlations, particularly in SPI indices. For **SPI3**, the raw correlation is **r = 0.051 (p = 0.260)**, while the partial correlation is **r = 0.018 (p = 0.685)**, with only **36.0%** independence. For **SPI6**, the raw correlation is **r = 0.032 (p = 0.476)**, while the partial correlation is **r = −0.004 (p = 0.927)**, with only **12.8%** independence (and a sign change). For **SPEI3**, the raw correlation is **r = 0.125 (p = 0.006)**, while the partial correlation is **r = 0.090 (p = 0.046)**, with **72.1%** independence. For **SPEI6**, the raw correlation is **r = 0.125 (p = 0.006)**, while the partial correlation is **r = 0.089 (p = 0.048)**, with **71.3%** independence. The low independence in SPI indices suggests strong ENSO confounding, while the moderate independence in SPEI indices suggests that evaporative demand effects may contribute to a more independent IOD signal. The drought–Niño3.4 correlations are positive and moderate (**r = 0.203 to 0.243**), indicating that ENSO and drought are positively related in North Africa, contributing to the ENSO confounding.

#### 3.5.2 Index-specific patterns

**SPI3 vs SPI6:** Comparing 3-month and 6-month precipitation indices reveals that SPI6 generally shows higher independence from ENSO than SPI3. In East Africa, SPI6 shows **88.0%** independence vs **80.0%** in SPI3. In West Africa, SPI6 shows **80.4%** independence vs **71.7%** in SPI3. In Southern Africa, SPI6 shows very low independence (**4.5%**) vs a sign change in SPI3. In Central Africa, both show high independence (>100%). In North Africa, SPI6 shows very low independence (**12.8%**) vs **36.0%** in SPI3. The 6-month accumulation window may capture cumulative effects that are less influenced by ENSO co-variability.

**SPEI3 vs SPEI6:** Similar to SPI, SPEI6 generally shows similar or slightly higher independence than SPEI3. In East Africa, SPEI6 shows **86.7%** independence vs **83.9%** in SPEI3. In West Africa, SPEI6 shows **81.1%** independence vs **58.0%** in SPEI3. In Southern Africa, SPEI6 shows **42.3%** independence vs **34.2%** in SPEI3. In Central Africa, both show high independence (>98%). In North Africa, SPEI6 shows **71.3%** independence vs **72.1%** in SPEI3. The 6-month accumulation window in water balance terms shows similar patterns to precipitation indices.

**SPI vs SPEI:** Comparing precipitation-only (SPI) and water balance (SPEI) indices reveals important differences in ENSO independence. In East Africa, both SPI and SPEI show high independence (80–88%), with similar patterns. In Southern Africa, SPEI indices show higher independence (**34.2–42.3%**) than SPI indices (**4.5%** or sign changes), suggesting that evaporative demand effects may contribute to a more independent IOD signal. In West Africa, SPI indices show higher independence (**71.7–80.4%**) than SPEI3 (**58.0%**), though SPEI6 shows similar independence (**81.1%**). In Central Africa, both SPI and SPEI show high independence (>98%). In North Africa, SPEI indices show much higher independence (**71.3–72.1%**) than SPI indices (**12.8–36.0%**), indicating that evaporative demand effects are less influenced by ENSO co-variability than precipitation effects.

#### 3.5.3 ENSO co-variability patterns

**DMI–Niño3.4 correlations:** The correlation between DMI and Niño3.4 is **r = 0.163** across all regions and indices, reflecting the known co-occurrence of IOD and ENSO events. This moderate positive correlation indicates that positive IOD events tend to co-occur with El Niño conditions, while negative IOD events tend to co-occur with La Niña conditions, consistent with the phase separation observed in ENSO-stratified composites (Section 3.3).

**Drought–Niño3.4 correlations:** The correlations between drought indices and Niño3.4 vary substantially by region, reflecting regional differences in ENSO teleconnections. In **East Africa**, drought–Niño3.4 correlations are positive (**r = 0.169 to 0.239**), indicating that El Niño conditions are associated with wetter conditions (positive drought index values). In **Southern Africa**, drought–Niño3.4 correlations are negative (**r = −0.161 to −0.203**), indicating that El Niño conditions are associated with drier conditions (negative drought index values). In **West Africa**, drought–Niño3.4 correlations are weak negative (**r = −0.046 to −0.112**), indicating minimal direct ENSO influence. In **Central Africa**, drought–Niño3.4 correlations are very weak (**r = −0.056 to 0.009**), indicating minimal ENSO influence. In **North Africa**, drought–Niño3.4 correlations are positive (**r = 0.203 to 0.243**), indicating that El Niño conditions are associated with wetter conditions.

The regional patterns in drought–Niño3.4 correlations explain the varying degrees of ENSO independence observed in partial correlations. Regions with strong drought–ENSO relationships (e.g., Southern Africa with negative correlations, North Africa with positive correlations) show lower IOD independence, while regions with weak drought–ENSO relationships (e.g., Central Africa) show higher IOD independence.

#### 3.5.4 Interpretation and implications

**High independence regions (East Africa, Central Africa):** Regions showing high IOD independence (80–100%+) support direct IOD–drought teleconnections that are largely independent of ENSO. In East Africa, the high independence (80–88%) combined with strong raw correlations (r = 0.161–0.208) provides robust evidence for a direct IOD influence on drought. In Central Africa, the high independence (>98%) combined with weak raw correlations suggests that IOD has a small but independent influence, though the weak correlations limit practical significance.

**Low independence regions (Southern Africa, North Africa in SPI indices):** Regions showing low IOD independence (<50%) suggest that apparent IOD–drought relationships are largely explained by ENSO co-variability. In Southern Africa, the very low independence in SPI6 (**4.5%**) indicates that the apparent IOD–drought relationship is almost entirely due to ENSO confounding. In North Africa, the low independence in SPI indices (**12.8–36.0%**) suggests strong ENSO confounding, though SPEI indices show moderate independence (**71.3–72.1%**), indicating that evaporative demand effects may be more independent.

**Moderate independence regions (West Africa, North Africa in SPEI indices):** Regions showing moderate independence (50–80%) suggest that IOD has a partially independent influence, with both direct IOD forcing and ENSO co-variability contributing to the observed relationships. The moderate independence indicates that IOD–drought relationships in these regions reflect a combination of direct IOD teleconnections and shared ENSO forcing.

**Forecasting implications:** The partial correlation results have important implications for operational drought forecasting. In regions with high IOD independence (East Africa, Central Africa), IOD can be used as a predictor with minimal concern about ENSO confounding. In regions with low IOD independence (Southern Africa, North Africa in SPI indices), IOD may be less useful as an independent predictor, and forecasters should consider ENSO state when interpreting IOD signals. In regions with moderate independence, both IOD and ENSO should be considered jointly in forecasting frameworks.

### 3.6 Seasonal dependence

Seasonal correlation analyses reveal that IOD–drought relationships exhibit strong seasonal variations across Africa, with patterns that differ substantially from the annual or SON-only relationships. The analysis examines correlations between DMI and drought indices separately for each season (DJF, MAM, JJA, SON), revealing when IOD has the strongest influence on African drought and how this varies by region and index type.

#### 3.6.1 Regional seasonal patterns

**East Africa:** East Africa shows the strongest seasonal correlations, with consistent positive relationships during DJF and SON across all indices. For **SPI3**, correlations are: **DJF r = 0.308 (p < 0.001)**, **MAM r = −0.262 (p = 0.003)**, **JJA r = 0.128 (p = 0.157)**, and **SON r = 0.504 (p < 0.001)**. The SON correlation is the strongest, consistent with IOD peaking during SON and East African short rains occurring during this season. The negative MAM correlation suggests that positive IOD events may be associated with reduced precipitation during the long rains season. For **SPI6**, the pattern is similar but with stronger DJF correlations (**r = 0.406, p < 0.001**) and weaker MAM correlations (**r = −0.004, p = 0.968**). For **SPEI3**, correlations are: **DJF r = 0.375 (p < 0.001)**, **MAM r = −0.189 (p = 0.036)**, **JJA r = 0.060 (p = 0.511)**, and **SON r = 0.429 (p < 0.001)**. For **SPEI6**, correlations are: **DJF r = 0.427 (p < 0.001)**, **MAM r = 0.070 (p = 0.440)**, **JJA r = −0.047 (p = 0.605)**, and **SON r = 0.307 (p < 0.001)**. The 6-month accumulation window in SPEI6 shows weaker SON correlations compared to SPEI3, reflecting how cumulative effects may dilute seasonal signals.

**Southern Africa:** Southern Africa shows a distinctly different seasonal pattern, with weak or non-significant correlations during most seasons, but significant correlations during specific seasons that vary by index. For **SPI3**, correlations are: **DJF r = 0.013 (p = 0.887)**, **MAM r = −0.169 (p = 0.062)**, **JJA r = −0.067 (p = 0.463)**, and **SON r = 0.286 (p = 0.001)**. Only SON shows a significant positive correlation, contrary to expectations that MAM would show the strongest negative correlation. For **SPI6**, correlations are: **DJF r = 0.078 (p = 0.392)**, **MAM r = −0.099 (p = 0.274)**, **JJA r = −0.247 (p = 0.006)**, and **SON r = 0.180 (p = 0.047)**. Both JJA and SON show significant correlations, with JJA showing a stronger negative correlation than MAM. For **SPEI3**, correlations are: **DJF r = 0.069 (p = 0.447)**, **MAM r = −0.199 (p = 0.027)**, **JJA r = −0.173 (p = 0.055)**, and **SON r = 0.275 (p = 0.002)**. MAM shows a significant negative correlation in SPEI3, suggesting that evaporative demand effects contribute to drought during MAM. For **SPEI6**, correlations are: **DJF r = 0.140 (p = 0.121)**, **MAM r = −0.097 (p = 0.285)**, **JJA r = −0.241 (p = 0.007)**, and **SON r = 0.166 (p = 0.067)**. Only JJA shows a significant negative correlation in SPEI6. The seasonal pattern in Southern Africa suggests that IOD–drought relationships are most apparent during JJA (winter) and SON (spring), with MAM showing significant negative correlations only in SPEI3. This pattern aligns with the lag correlation results showing ±9-month lags, suggesting that IOD impacts on Southern Africa may be delayed by approximately one seasonal cycle.

**West Africa:** West Africa shows negative correlations during MAM across all indices, suggesting that positive IOD events are associated with reduced drought (wetter conditions) during this season. For **SPI3**, correlations are: **DJF r = −0.023 (p = 0.798)**, **MAM r = −0.234 (p = 0.009)**, **JJA r = 0.007 (p = 0.935)**, and **SON r = −0.003 (p = 0.973)**. Only MAM shows a significant negative correlation. For **SPI6**, correlations are: **DJF r = −0.121 (p = 0.184)**, **MAM r = −0.278 (p = 0.002)**, **JJA r = −0.041 (p = 0.649)**, and **SON r = −0.068 (p = 0.452)**. MAM shows a stronger significant negative correlation in SPI6. For **SPEI3**, correlations are: **DJF r = 0.095 (p = 0.298)**, **MAM r = −0.272 (p = 0.002)**, **JJA r = 0.028 (p = 0.758)**, and **SON r = 0.034 (p = 0.706)**. MAM shows a significant negative correlation, while other seasons show weak or non-significant correlations. For **SPEI6**, correlations are: **DJF r = −0.062 (p = 0.493)**, **MAM r = −0.264 (p = 0.003)**, **JJA r = −0.020 (p = 0.824)**, and **SON r = −0.037 (p = 0.685)**. MAM shows a significant negative correlation, consistent with other indices. The consistent negative MAM correlations in West Africa suggest that positive IOD events enhance precipitation during the West African monsoon season, reducing drought risk.

**Central Africa:** Central Africa shows mixed seasonal patterns with significant correlations during MAM and SON. For **SPI3**, correlations are: **DJF r = 0.073 (p = 0.421)**, **MAM r = −0.291 (p = 0.001)**, **JJA r = 0.095 (p = 0.296)**, and **SON r = 0.226 (p = 0.012)**. Both MAM and SON show significant correlations, with MAM showing a negative correlation and SON showing a positive correlation. For **SPI6**, correlations are: **DJF r = 0.097 (p = 0.285)**, **MAM r = −0.197 (p = 0.029)**, **JJA r = −0.036 (p = 0.696)**, and **SON r = 0.116 (p = 0.201)**. Only MAM shows a significant negative correlation. For **SPEI3**, correlations are: **DJF r = 0.195 (p = 0.031)**, **MAM r = −0.288 (p = 0.001)**, **JJA r = 0.096 (p = 0.290)**, and **SON r = 0.244 (p = 0.007)**. DJF, MAM, and SON all show significant correlations, with MAM negative and DJF/SON positive. For **SPEI6**, correlations are: **DJF r = 0.132 (p = 0.147)**, **MAM r = −0.176 (p = 0.052)**, **JJA r = −0.022 (p = 0.808)**, and **SON r = 0.132 (p = 0.147)**. No seasons show significant correlations in SPEI6. The seasonal pattern in Central Africa suggests that IOD has opposing effects during MAM (negative) and SON (positive), with evaporative demand effects (SPEI3) showing stronger signals than precipitation alone (SPI3).

**North Africa:** North Africa shows weak seasonal correlations overall, with significant correlations appearing only in SPEI indices. For **SPI3**, correlations are: **DJF r = 0.082 (p = 0.367)**, **MAM r = −0.062 (p = 0.499)**, **JJA r = −0.044 (p = 0.630)**, and **SON r = 0.107 (p = 0.238)**. No seasons show significant correlations. For **SPI6**, correlations are: **DJF r = 0.029 (p = 0.751)**, **MAM r = −0.152 (p = 0.093)**, **JJA r = −0.033 (p = 0.715)**, and **SON r = 0.114 (p = 0.209)**. No seasons show significant correlations. For **SPEI3**, correlations are: **DJF r = 0.204 (p = 0.024)**, **MAM r = −0.010 (p = 0.912)**, **JJA r = 0.047 (p = 0.602)**, and **SON r = 0.194 (p = 0.031)**. Both DJF and SON show significant positive correlations. For **SPEI6**, correlations are: **DJF r = 0.185 (p = 0.040)**, **MAM r = −0.035 (p = 0.699)**, **JJA r = 0.100 (p = 0.273)**, and **SON r = 0.203 (p = 0.025)**. Both DJF and SON show significant positive correlations. The stronger correlations in SPEI indices compared to SPI indices in North Africa suggest that evaporative demand is a key mechanism linking IOD to drought in this region, with positive correlations during DJF and SON indicating that positive IOD events enhance water availability.

#### 3.6.2 Index-specific seasonal patterns

**SPI3 vs SPI6:** Comparing 3-month and 6-month precipitation indices reveals how accumulation windows affect seasonal correlations. In East Africa, SPI6 shows stronger DJF correlations (0.406 vs 0.308) but weaker SON correlations (0.352 vs 0.504), suggesting that cumulative effects enhance DJF signals but may dilute SON signals. In Southern Africa, SPI6 shows significant JJA correlations (−0.247) that are not significant in SPI3, suggesting that the 6-month accumulation captures delayed effects. In West Africa, SPI6 shows stronger MAM negative correlations (−0.278 vs −0.234), reflecting cumulative effects. In Central Africa, SPI6 shows weaker correlations overall, with only MAM remaining significant.

**SPEI3 vs SPEI6:** Similar to SPI, SPEI6 shows different seasonal patterns compared to SPEI3. In East Africa, SPEI6 shows stronger DJF correlations (0.427 vs 0.375) but weaker SON correlations (0.307 vs 0.429). In Southern Africa, SPEI6 shows significant JJA correlations (−0.241) that are not significant in SPEI3, while SPEI3 shows significant MAM correlations (−0.199) that are not significant in SPEI6. In West Africa, both SPEI3 and SPEI6 show similar MAM negative correlations. In Central Africa, SPEI3 shows significant correlations in DJF, MAM, and SON, while SPEI6 shows no significant correlations, suggesting that the 6-month accumulation may dilute seasonal signals in this region.

**SPI vs SPEI:** Comparing precipitation-only (SPI) and water balance (SPEI) indices reveals how evaporative demand affects seasonal correlations. In East Africa, SPEI indices show similar patterns to SPI indices, with both showing strong SON correlations. In Southern Africa, SPEI3 shows a significant MAM negative correlation (−0.199) that is not significant in SPI3 (−0.169, p = 0.062), suggesting that evaporative demand contributes to drought during MAM. In West Africa, both SPI and SPEI show similar MAM negative correlations. In Central Africa, SPEI3 shows significant DJF correlations (0.195) that are not significant in SPI3 (0.073), suggesting that evaporative demand effects are important during DJF. In North Africa, SPEI indices show significant DJF and SON correlations that are not significant in SPI indices, indicating that evaporative demand is a key mechanism in this region.

#### 3.6.3 Seasonal lag relationships and forecasting implications

The seasonal correlation patterns reveal important insights for drought forecasting. In **East Africa**, the strong SON correlations (r = 0.504 for SPI3, r = 0.429 for SPEI3) align with IOD peaking during SON and East African short rains occurring during this season, providing concurrent prediction potential. The strong DJF correlations (r = 0.406 for SPI6, r = 0.427 for SPEI6) suggest that IOD can provide 1–3 month lead time for DJF conditions.

In **Southern Africa**, the significant SON correlations (r = 0.286 for SPI3, r = 0.275 for SPEI3) combined with the lag correlation results showing ±8–9 month lags suggest that IOD during SON can provide early warning for conditions approximately 6–9 months later. The seasonal correlation results reveal that the delayed response manifests most strongly during **JJA (winter)** rather than MAM (autumn). JJA shows significant negative correlations in SPI6 (r = −0.247, p = 0.006) and SPEI6 (r = −0.241, p = 0.007), with stronger magnitudes than MAM correlations. MAM shows a significant negative correlation only in SPEI3 (r = −0.199, p = 0.027), while MAM correlations are not significant in SPI3 (r = −0.169, p = 0.062), SPI6 (r = −0.099, p = 0.274), or SPEI6 (r = −0.097, p = 0.285). This pattern indicates that the delayed IOD impact on Southern Africa peaks during JJA (approximately 7–9 months after SON), providing a **6–9 month lead time** for drought forecasting. The stronger correlations in 6-month accumulation indices (SPI6, SPEI6) during JJA suggest that cumulative effects over the delayed period are important, with the 6-month window capturing both the initial IOD forcing and subsequent persistence mechanisms. Forecasters can therefore use IOD signals during SON as an early warning indicator for potential drought conditions in Southern Africa during the following JJA season, giving sufficient lead time (6–9 months) for preparation and planning.

In **West Africa**, the consistent negative MAM correlations across all indices (r = −0.234 to −0.278) suggest that positive IOD events enhance precipitation during the West African monsoon season, providing potential for seasonal-scale prediction. The negative correlations indicate that positive IOD is associated with reduced drought risk (wetter conditions) during MAM.

The seasonal patterns highlight that IOD–drought relationships are not confined to SON, when IOD peaks, but extend across multiple seasons with varying strengths and signs. This seasonal dependence reflects the complex interactions between IOD-related circulation anomalies, regional rainfall regimes, seasonal migration of convergence zones, and the evolution of land–atmosphere feedbacks over time.

### 3.8 Physical consistency from ERA5 circulation composites

ERA5 circulation composites during canonical IOD events provide mechanistic consistency for the drought teleconnections identified in the statistical analyses. The circulation anomalies reveal how IOD-related SST gradients reorganize atmospheric flow patterns, creating pathways for moisture transport anomalies that directly link oceanic forcing to African drought conditions. The analysis focuses on three key atmospheric levels: surface pressure (MSLP), low-level winds (850 hPa), and mid-tropospheric height (500 hPa), each providing complementary information about the physical mechanisms.

#### 3.8.1 Mean sea-level pressure (MSLP) anomalies

**Positive IOD events:** During positive IOD events, MSLP composites reveal a reorganization of surface pressure patterns that supports the drought anomalies observed in the statistical analyses. Anomalous high pressure develops over the **southwestern Indian Ocean and Southern Africa**, creating a pressure gradient that alters convergence patterns and suppresses convective activity. This high-pressure anomaly is consistent with the suppressed convection over the eastern Indian Ocean during positive IOD events, as cooler SSTs reduce surface heating and atmospheric instability. The high-pressure anomaly over Southern Africa aligns with the negative drought index anomalies (drier conditions) observed in Southern Africa during positive IOD events, particularly in SPI6 and SPEI6 composites (Section 3.2). The pressure pattern creates subsidence and divergence at the surface, inhibiting cloud formation and precipitation.

Over **East Africa**, MSLP anomalies during positive IOD events show a more complex pattern, with slight pressure increases that may contribute to the moderate negative drought anomalies observed in some indices. The pressure anomalies are weaker than over Southern Africa, consistent with the more moderate drought responses in East Africa compared to Southern Africa. Over **West Africa and Central Africa**, MSLP anomalies are generally weak, reflecting the weaker IOD–drought relationships in these regions.

**Negative IOD events:** During negative IOD events, MSLP composites show the reverse pattern, with anomalous low pressure over the southwestern Indian Ocean and Southern Africa. This low-pressure anomaly enhances convergence and supports increased convective activity, consistent with the positive drought index anomalies (wetter conditions) observed in Southern Africa during negative IOD events. The pressure pattern creates surface convergence and upward motion, favoring cloud formation and precipitation. Over East Africa, negative IOD events show slight pressure decreases, supporting the positive drought index anomalies (wetter conditions) observed in East Africa.

**Physical consistency:** The MSLP anomaly patterns are physically consistent with the drought composite results. Regions showing stronger pressure anomalies (Southern Africa) correspond to regions with stronger drought responses, while regions with weaker pressure anomalies (West Africa, Central Africa) show weaker drought responses. The pressure anomalies create the surface convergence/divergence patterns that directly influence precipitation, providing a mechanistic link between IOD SST anomalies and African drought conditions.

#### 3.8.2 850 hPa wind anomalies and moisture transport

**Positive IOD events:** At 850 hPa, wind anomalies during positive IOD events reveal critical changes in low-level moisture transport pathways that explain the regional drought patterns. Over the **equatorial Indian Ocean**, anomalous **easterly winds** develop, reversing the normal westerly flow during SON. This easterly anomaly is driven by the enhanced pressure gradient between the cooler western Indian Ocean (higher pressure) and the warmer eastern Indian Ocean (lower pressure) during positive IOD events. The easterly winds reduce moisture transport from the Indian Ocean toward East Africa, creating a **moisture deficit** that directly supports the negative drought index anomalies (drier conditions) observed in East Africa.

Over **Southern Africa**, wind anomalies show an anomalous **anticyclonic circulation** pattern, with clockwise flow around a high-pressure center. This anticyclonic circulation creates **divergent flow** at low levels, reducing moisture convergence and suppressing precipitation. The wind pattern is consistent with the high-pressure anomaly observed in MSLP composites and supports the negative drought index anomalies in Southern Africa. The anticyclonic circulation also creates **offshore flow** along the eastern coast of Southern Africa, further reducing moisture availability from the Indian Ocean.

Over **West Africa**, wind anomalies show weak **westerly anomalies** that may enhance moisture transport from the Atlantic Ocean during positive IOD events. This pattern is consistent with the weak positive drought index anomalies (wetter conditions) observed in some West African composites, suggesting that positive IOD events may enhance Atlantic moisture transport to West Africa, partially offsetting the Indian Ocean moisture deficit.

**Negative IOD events:** During negative IOD events, wind anomalies show the reverse pattern. Over the **equatorial Indian Ocean**, anomalous **westerly winds** develop, enhancing the normal westerly flow and increasing moisture transport toward East Africa. This westerly anomaly is driven by the reversed pressure gradient during negative IOD events (warmer western Indian Ocean, cooler eastern Indian Ocean). The enhanced westerly flow creates a **moisture surplus** that supports the positive drought index anomalies (wetter conditions) observed in East Africa.

Over **Southern Africa**, wind anomalies show an anomalous **cyclonic circulation** pattern, with counterclockwise flow around a low-pressure center. This cyclonic circulation creates **convergent flow** at low levels, enhancing moisture convergence and supporting precipitation. The wind pattern is consistent with the low-pressure anomaly observed in MSLP composites and supports the positive drought index anomalies in Southern Africa. The cyclonic circulation also creates **onshore flow** along the eastern coast, enhancing moisture availability from the Indian Ocean.

**Moisture transport pathways:** The 850 hPa wind anomalies reveal distinct moisture transport pathways that connect IOD SST anomalies to African drought conditions. During positive IOD events, the reduced Indian Ocean moisture transport to East Africa creates a direct pathway for drought development. The anticyclonic circulation over Southern Africa creates a secondary pathway through reduced convergence and increased divergence. During negative IOD events, the enhanced Indian Ocean moisture transport to East Africa and the cyclonic circulation over Southern Africa create pathways for increased precipitation and reduced drought risk.

**Physical consistency:** The 850 hPa wind anomalies are physically consistent with both the MSLP patterns and the drought composite results. The wind anomalies directly explain the moisture transport changes that drive precipitation anomalies, providing a mechanistic link between IOD forcing and drought responses. The regional patterns in wind anomalies (strongest over East Africa and Southern Africa, weaker over West Africa and Central Africa) match the regional patterns in drought responses, supporting the physical consistency of the teleconnection mechanisms.

#### 3.8.3 500 hPa geopotential height anomalies and vertical motion

**Positive IOD events:** At 500 hPa, geopotential height anomalies during positive IOD events reveal mid-tropospheric circulation patterns that support the surface pressure and wind anomalies. Over **Southern Africa and the southwestern Indian Ocean**, anomalous **high geopotential height** (ridge) develops, indicating subsidence and suppressed vertical motion. This ridge pattern is consistent with the high-pressure anomaly at the surface and the anticyclonic circulation at 850 hPa, creating a **vertically coherent circulation structure** that inhibits precipitation. The ridge extends across the Southern Hemisphere, suggesting a large-scale circulation response to IOD forcing.

Over **East Africa**, geopotential height anomalies show a weaker ridge pattern, consistent with the more moderate drought responses in this region. The ridge creates subsidence that suppresses convection, supporting the negative drought index anomalies. Over the **equatorial Indian Ocean**, geopotential height anomalies show a **trough** pattern, indicating enhanced vertical motion and convection over the warmer eastern Indian Ocean during positive IOD events. This trough pattern is consistent with the enhanced convection over the eastern Indian Ocean that characterizes positive IOD events.

**Negative IOD events:** During negative IOD events, geopotential height anomalies show the reverse pattern. Over **Southern Africa and the southwestern Indian Ocean**, anomalous **low geopotential height** (trough) develops, indicating upward motion and enhanced convection. This trough pattern is consistent with the low-pressure anomaly at the surface and the cyclonic circulation at 850 hPa, creating a vertically coherent circulation structure that supports precipitation. The trough extends across the Southern Hemisphere, suggesting a large-scale circulation response.

Over **East Africa**, geopotential height anomalies show a weaker trough pattern, consistent with the positive drought index anomalies (wetter conditions). The trough creates upward motion that enhances convection, supporting increased precipitation. Over the **equatorial Indian Ocean**, geopotential height anomalies show a **ridge** pattern, indicating suppressed vertical motion over the cooler eastern Indian Ocean during negative IOD events.

**Vertical motion and precipitation:** The 500 hPa geopotential height anomalies directly indicate vertical motion patterns that influence precipitation. Positive height anomalies (ridges) indicate subsidence, which suppresses convection and precipitation, while negative height anomalies (troughs) indicate upward motion, which enhances convection and precipitation. The height anomalies are physically consistent with the surface pressure and low-level wind patterns, creating a three-dimensional circulation structure that explains the drought responses.

**Wave train patterns:** The hemispheric extent of the geopotential height anomalies suggests that IOD forcing may excite **Rossby wave trains** that propagate across the Southern Hemisphere. These wave trains can create remote teleconnections that influence African circulation patterns beyond the direct Indian Ocean influence. The wave train patterns may explain some of the regional differences in drought responses, as different regions may be influenced by different phases of the wave train.

**Physical consistency:** The 500 hPa geopotential height anomalies are physically consistent with both the surface and low-level circulation patterns and the drought composite results. The vertically coherent circulation structure (high pressure at surface, anticyclonic flow at 850 hPa, ridge at 500 hPa during positive IOD events) creates a complete mechanistic pathway from IOD SST anomalies to African drought conditions. The height anomalies provide the vertical motion component that directly influences precipitation, completing the physical chain from oceanic forcing to drought responses.

#### 3.8.4 Integrated physical mechanism

The ERA5 circulation composites reveal an **integrated physical mechanism** that connects IOD SST anomalies to African drought conditions through a three-dimensional circulation response:

1. **SST forcing:** Positive IOD events create cooler SSTs in the western Indian Ocean and warmer SSTs in the eastern Indian Ocean, reversing the normal SST gradient.

2. **Convection response:** The SST gradient reorganizes convection patterns, with enhanced convection over the warmer eastern Indian Ocean and suppressed convection over the cooler western Indian Ocean during positive IOD events.

3. **Pressure response:** The convection changes create surface pressure anomalies, with high pressure over the southwestern Indian Ocean and Southern Africa during positive IOD events.

4. **Wind response:** The pressure anomalies drive low-level wind changes, with easterly anomalies over the equatorial Indian Ocean reducing moisture transport to East Africa, and anticyclonic circulation over Southern Africa creating divergent flow.

5. **Vertical motion response:** The pressure and wind patterns create mid-tropospheric height anomalies, with ridges indicating subsidence and suppressed precipitation, and troughs indicating upward motion and enhanced precipitation.

6. **Precipitation and drought response:** The integrated circulation response creates precipitation anomalies that directly translate into drought index anomalies, with reduced precipitation (negative drought indices) during positive IOD events in East Africa and Southern Africa, and increased precipitation (positive drought indices) during negative IOD events.

**Regional consistency:** The circulation composites show strong physical consistency with the drought composite results. Regions with stronger circulation anomalies (East Africa, Southern Africa) show stronger drought responses, while regions with weaker circulation anomalies (West Africa, Central Africa) show weaker drought responses. The circulation patterns directly explain the moisture transport changes that drive precipitation anomalies, providing mechanistic support for the statistical associations identified in the drought composites, lag correlations, and partial correlations.

**ENSO interactions:** The circulation composites also provide insight into ENSO–IOD interactions. When IOD and ENSO are in phase (e.g., positive IOD with El Niño), the circulation anomalies may be amplified, creating stronger drought responses. When IOD and ENSO are out of phase, the circulation anomalies may be weakened or reversed, explaining the conditional dependence observed in ENSO-stratified composites (Section 3.3). The circulation patterns reveal how ENSO modulates IOD teleconnections through shared circulation pathways.

**Temporal evolution:** The circulation composites during SON (IOD peak season) provide a snapshot of the circulation state that influences subsequent drought conditions. The lag correlation results (Section 3.4) showing 2–4 month lead times suggest that the circulation anomalies established during SON persist and influence precipitation patterns over the following months. The circulation composites capture the initial forcing that drives the delayed drought responses observed in the lag correlations.

To improve interpretability, geopotential height anomalies and wind vectors are plotted separately, reducing visual clutter compared to overlaid products. Hemispheric and polar-view geopotential height plots support interpretation of broader circulation structures beyond the African continent, revealing the large-scale wave train patterns that connect IOD forcing to remote African teleconnections.

## 4. Discussion
### 4.1 Interpreting regional heterogeneity
The spatial heterogeneity of IOD–drought responses across Africa likely reflects differences in moisture source regions, the seasonal timing of rainfall, and the pathways by which Indian Ocean convection anomalies project onto African circulation. Regions directly influenced by Indian Ocean moisture transport are expected to show stronger sensitivity during seasons aligned with IOD peak activity. Elsewhere, teleconnections may arise through broader circulation shifts at mid‑levels, changes in subtropical highs, or synoptic-scale steering effects.

### 4.2 ENSO–IOD interaction and conditional effects
ENSO-stratified results indicate that IOD impacts cannot be interpreted independently of the Indo-Pacific background state. ENSO can alter convection over the Maritime Continent and Indian Ocean, shifting the Walker circulation and therefore modulating IOD-related anomalies. This conditional dependence implies that forecast systems and attribution studies should consider both modes jointly rather than treating DMI as an isolated predictor.

### 4.3 SPI vs SPEI and the role of evaporative demand
Differences between SPI and SPEI composites suggest that evaporative demand (and temperature anomalies) can amplify drought signals in some regions and seasons. As warming intensifies, SPEI may increasingly diverge from SPI, particularly in semi‑arid zones where evaporative demand contributes strongly to water deficits. Future work should examine whether the observed differences strengthen over time and how they relate to long‑term trends in temperature and potential evapotranspiration.

### 4.4 Uncertainty and limitations
Key limitations include limited event counts for certain ENSO×IOD combinations, spatial autocorrelation affecting significance interpretation, and sensitivity to thresholds used for event selection and ENSO classification. Additionally, reproducibility may depend on consistent scientific Python environments (e.g., xarray/dask version compatibility for older serialized outputs). These limitations can be addressed through expanded sensitivity analysis (varying thresholds), spatial multiple-testing correction, and replication across alternate datasets and reanalyses.

## 5. Conclusions
Across 1980–2020, IOD phase is associated with spatially heterogeneous drought anomalies across Africa, with responses that vary by drought metric (SPI vs SPEI), accumulation window, and season. ENSO significantly modulates IOD–drought relationships, underscoring the need for conditional or multivariate frameworks. ERA5 circulation composites support mechanistic consistency, indicating that IOD events are associated with changes in low-level wind anomalies and mid‑tropospheric height patterns that plausibly influence moisture transport and rainfall. Collectively, the results provide a coherent statistical–physical basis for incorporating IOD information into drought early warning and seasonal outlook guidance over Africa.
